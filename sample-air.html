Whiteness is so Common, it’s Like Seeing Through Air: Using Critical Media Literacy to See Through Hegemonic Whiteness in the 2023 Film Air

Rich Riddle · Curriculum and instruction seminar, Fall 2024

Why this piece

I wrote this paper for a graduate curriculum and instruction seminar, and it became the writing sample I submitted with my Ph.D. application. It uses critical media literacy, a set of questions about who made a message, what it leaves out, and who benefits, to read the 2023 film Air. Those same questions run through my teaching: my students and I read popular film and digital media the way we read any other text, asking what it puts at the center and what it pushes to the edges.

You have an amazing capacity for self-deception; how do you do that?

The 2023 film Air tells the story of Nike's groundbreaking deal with Michael Jordan in 1984 to create the Air Jordan shoe line. While on the surface, the film seems to celebrate Jordan's talent and Nike's vision, a critical analysis reveals how the film reinforces problematic racial ideologies through its narrative choices and character portrayals. In this paper, I will establish what race means, particularly how media produces institutionalized racism, and how critical media literacy can help see through these constructs to uncover deeper meaning. This paper will examine how Air upholds existing racial hierarchies, despite its surface-level celebration of a Black athlete’s success, by analyzing its use of white savior tropes, character portrayals, and narrative perspective through a framework of critical media literacy and when available the intertextualities that exist when reproducing a historical film based on a true story.

Even when a film’s racist content is obvious, it’s worth examining why the movie industry—an institution by nature—continues to produce such works. This raises the question: Why is this type of analysis still needed in 2024? How, in a society that some claim to be “post-racial,” do films like Air continue to be made (Goodman, 2020)? I am reminded of a quote from Battlestar Galactica (Moore, 2003): “You have an amazing capacity for self-deception; how do you do that?” In the analysis of Air, the question seems, 20 years later, still appropriate within a media context. How does society continue in this mass-effect of self-deception?

This tendency to avoid acknowledging one’s role in harm, particularly in systems of oppression, mirrors how those with racial privilege often distance themselves from complicity in upholding racial hierarchies. In Air, this self-deception is subtly embedded in the narrative choices that place white figures at the center of a Black athlete’s story. This distancing of responsibility often occurs in systems that enforce and maintain racism, and media—especially in a world of ever-increasing use, reliance, and access—is no exception. As a powerful social institution, media shapes racial ideologies, often reinforcing the self-deception that racial hierarchies are natural or inevitable. This self-deception perpetuated by media extends beyond individual narratives to broader societal constructs, particularly in the understanding and portrayal of race itself.

Framework: Race and Critical Media Literacy

Race, then—a social construct—is not originally a biological or genetic reality, but a manufactured one (Funk, 2016). Despite attempts in the 19th and 20th centuries, eugenics largely failed in its efforts to eliminate what it considered undesirables, but was successful in creating racial hierarchies in the U.S. Although similar ideologies had devastating consequences elsewhere, such as in Germany, the main aims in the U.S. had tragic, but limited effects (e.g. sterilization of certain groups) (Goodman, 2020). Nevertheless, race has profound implications as a categorization system that creates and maintains hierarchies of power and privilege. As Goodman, et al. argue in Race: Are We So Different?, race was invented relatively recently as a tool to justify exploitation, oppression, and social control by categorizing people into groups seen as either "primitive" and "savage" or "advanced" and "civilized” (2020). These ideologies, designed to establish and enforce dominance, persist in our society and impact lived experiences globally.

While the origins of race as a social construct are rooted in historical attempts at justifying exploitation and oppression, the ongoing presence of these ideas continues through various social mechanisms. Racial ideologies—sets of beliefs and assumptions about racial groups—are produced and reinforced through social institutions, particularly in media representations. Media plays a crucial role in normalizing these ideologies, transforming rhetoric into lived experience. Although discriminatory laws have largely been repealed, the attitudes that sustain inequity remain embedded within social systems, adapting to new contexts in subtle forms. As Stuart Hall observes, “the ‘white eye’ may not be in the frame, but it is dictating everything within it,” highlighting how dominant, often invisible, perspectives shape what is seen and how it is interpreted (1981).

Given that race is a construct, then it becomes necessary to establish a way to systematically analyze it. Critical media literacy (CML) offers tools to analyze how media texts construct meaning and reinforce dominant ideologies, prompting researchers to challenge these representations (Funk 2016). CML extends beyond traditional literacy to examine who produces media messages, how they are constructed, and who benefits from these narratives. Funk, Kellner, and Share (2016) explain that CML is rooted in cultural studies and critical theory, drawing on frameworks from the Frankfurt and Birmingham Schools to question how media constructs reality for its audience. CML is a transformative pedagogy, encouraging active interrogation of media, which is particularly important in a media-saturated, digital world (Funk, 2016).

CML applied through a critical race lens highlights how media often perpetuates stereotypes and racist ideologies, especially when portraying people of color (Cammarota, 2011) and emphasizes the importance of identifying white savior narratives in films, which often portray people of color as only able to improve their lives through the guidance of white characters. By analyzing these portrayals, CML can question the intentions behind them and consider the broader social impact on racial perceptions. This approach requires moving beyond passive viewing to actively deconstruct the ideologies that support white, patriarchal hegemony and to understand how media representations reproduce racial hierarchies.

To guide CML analysis, several key questions help unpack the social implications of media messages. Who created the message, and what values or ideologies does it convey? How might different audiences interpret it, and what perspectives are foregrounded or omitted? Finally, who stands to benefit from this representation? This framework enables viewers to identify the intentions behind media production, question the underlying assumptions, and assess the broader impact on marginalized groups.

Analysis

White-Centered Music Choice

In the opening credits of Air, the film features Dire Straits' "Money for Nothing," a song by a British rock band with exclusively white members at the time. This choice is curious not only because of the song’s 1985 release date—making it anachronistic for a film set in 1984—but also because of the song’s thematic content and cultural associations. “Money for Nothing” dominated the charts for three consecutive weeks, and its themes of wealth and excess resonate with the Nike CEO's concerns about shareholder responses to the company's losses (Tucker, 1985). However, this scene also marks an important instance where white-centered cultural references overshadow the film’s potential to engage authentically with Black culture, a trend that persists in the film.

A closer examination of this choice using CML reveals how Air subtly upholds racial hierarchies by foregrounding white-centered narratives. According to music supervisor Andrea von Foerster, budget constraints played a significant role in the selection of "Money for Nothing" (Tangcay 2023). Although limited funding may justify the choice from a logistical standpoint, CML requires examination beyond stated goals, or any intentional fallacy.

After a review of the Billboard charts in 1983, 1984, and 1985 there were dozens of popular songs that trended on Billboard. Specifically, I located “Wanna Be Startin’ Somethin’” by Michael Jackson. Released in 1983, although with a less direct connection to consumerism, the idea presented in the film—this novel way to build a shoe line and brand an NBA player—would have been appropriate for song about starting something new. Although von Foerster discusses budgetary restraints, and certainly Michael Jackson’s discography is not inexpensive, it is not as though the music and sound teams were not creative in their efforts to secure rights to songs (Tangcay 2023).

As von Foerster had explained, “the film didn’t have a big budget for its music, so much of compiling the soundtrack was asking artists and music publishers to trust the film and its story. She says, “‘Time After Time’ was in the script, so we went for it, but we did not have the money that people think we had. It really was about trust and telling people this would be a great film.” von Foerster adds, “Apparently, Cyndi Lauper is a fan of Viola Davis. So she said, ‘Yes’” (Tangcay 2023).

Here is a clear example that, despite apparent difficulties, the team did seem to have the persuasive ability to secure music above its budget. Why could this approach not have been employed to use only, or predominantly Black artists? CML suggests that it was a matter of comfort with the use of popular white artists, and a potential reluctance to focus on Black artists, and possibly alienate white audiences by making the Air align too closely with a “hood” genre film.

Visual Omissions and White-Centered Framing

Following the choice of music, and simultaneously while a non-diegetic use of “Money for Nothing” plays, Air continues to establish its framing through the initial montage. During this montage, a series of images quickly defines the cultural and political atmosphere of 1984. The ten shots are overwhelming centered on whiteness, and make appeals to a white audience, even arguably, a right-leaning, racist white audience.

The very first shot of the montage establishes the year—1984—by pulling a popular clip from the 1983 Times Square countdown, using the WJKW-TV8 Cleveland news reel available on YouTube (videoholic1980sA, 2013). There are no Black partygoers available in this 3 minute and 40 second video, although there were a few seconds of the African-American girl group, The Pointer Sisters. What CML asks is: with the time available for this production, was there no video anywhere that displayed the Black experience of that New Year's Eve countdown and celebration? The very next image, however, moves from a potential oversight to a blatantly out-of-touch decision.

The second shot in the montage is of Ronald Reagan at the 1984 celebration at Century Plaza Hotel. An election where Reagan received 525 electoral votes (49 states), but starkly on 9% support from Black voters. His legacy was fraught with overtly racist remarks and actions that targeted African-American communities. In 1971, he was recorded referring to Africans as "monkeys," (Naftali, 2019) and as the Netflix documentary 13th argues, Reagan's “war on drugs” disproportionately affected and incarcerated Black men (Netflix, 2020).

Shown in this image, is a shot from the crowd, where he stood on stage to accept victory. Further, it was during this acceptance speech in 1984 when Reagan stated, “We fought many years for our principles, now we’ll work to keep those principles in practice. That’s what we have to leave to our children and to their children” (Reagan Library, 2016). But who are those children and to what principles did he really mean? Time magazine says, the racial wealth gap increased during Reagan years (the Reaganomics) and rolled back the effectiveness civil rights laws by defunding civil rights offices (2023). “As Princeton professor Keeanga-Yamahtta Taylor writes in The New Yorker, ‘Reagan mastered the art of color-blind racism in the post-civil-rights era with his invocations of ‘welfare queens’’” (Grihalva 2020). CML requires that I challenge a film that purports to be about the awesomeness of a Black athlete, and yet so blatantly uses an image of a man that began a pattern of dismantling support for Black Americans on a national level.

In this 12-second montage—which consists of 10 images—only three images feature African-Americans. Of these three, only two center African-Americans as the primary subjects of the shot. In the third shot, while Black athletes are present, Red Auerbach is placed center stage after his Boston Celtics' 1984 NBA Finals win. Auerbach, and the choice to place him, a white, wealthy coach and owner, as the primary subject while relegating Black athletes to the background mirrors Air's overall framing: white executives are central to the narrative while African-American athletes—including Michael Jordan himself—occupy peripheral roles. The initial montage reinforces the "white eye" perspective of Hall, presenting white individuals and their political, social, and professional concerns as central lenses through which this story is told.

Character Development, or Lack Thereof

As observed in previous sections on music and visual framing, Air continues to restrict Black characters to limited symbolic roles throughout its narrative. White character motivations are explored thoroughly throughout their interactions with each other and with Nike executives. Two of the main cast members, Sonny Vaccaro (Matt Damon) and Rob Strasser (Jason Bateman) are seen throughout the film, sharing bits of their personal lives, eating, sleeping, and in a scene that demonstrates how very close to the interior the audience is to the pair, they are seen in the men’s restroom, just after a meeting where Strasser states, “I got to shit.” But it is also during this impromptu meeting while the pair urinates and defecates, respectively—and far removed from anyone in the Jordan family—that critical parts of the solution continue to be discussed.

Although normal shot technique would be called over-the-shoulder, the shot here seems to be, more of an under-the-leg technique.

Further, at the outset, the film establishes Damon’s character, Sonny Vaccaro, as a gambler, implying that his pursuit of Jordan is a significant risk. This framing suggests that Vaccaro’s efforts represent an act of boldness and foresight, positioning him as a white “savior” who gambles for greatness by supporting Jordan. Additionally, it is just not just a matter of gambling, but one exploitation. Vaccaro states—and admits—25 minutes into the film that, “I don’t want to sign three players. I want to sign one. I want to sign [Jordan]. We build a shoe line around just him. We tap into something deeper, into the player’s identity. Into that [his confidence and talent]…he doesn’t wear the shoe: he is the shoe; the shoe is him.”

The narrative’s choice to acknowledge this exploitation and to still minimize Jordan’s role within the film, reducing his presence and enhancing the white characters’ impact, is an example of omission and lack of character development prevalent within this film. By focusing on white saviors like Vaccaro while sidelining Black figures like Jordan himself, Air creates a narrative that prioritizes white agency while objectifying Black talent. While the major omission of the film is an almost complete like of the actor portraying Michael Jordan, with almost no spoken lines and never once showing his face, even the most prominent Black character in Air, Jordan’s mom, Deloris, is herself quite limited.

As mentioned above, it was the inclusion of Viola Davis playing the role of Deloris Jordan that incentivized Lauper to permit her music in the film, at a presumably lower price than she would normally accept. I do hope she was not disappointed to discover, however, that out of a film with a runtime of 112 minutes, less than 23 minutes included Davis’s portrayal of Deloris. Beyond this statistical shortfall, CML also requires that I inspect Davis’s scenes just as I would Damon’s. What I found is a lack of interiority. Unlike the details of the lives shown of Damon and Bateman’s characters, and instead what is found is a dependence on and interaction with white characters. Even in interactions between Black characters, a white presence is consistently maintained, reinforcing Hall's concept of the 'white eye.' Throughout the film, there are no scenes where African-American characters interact without a white character present, effectively serving as an overseer. In this shot above, although at first glance there is a perception of balance and power during this initial meeting between Damon and Davis’s characters, on Davis’s side there is a dark, shadowy fence whereas on Damon’s side there are bright yellow flowers caught in what seems to be a ray of light.

Active Audience Reinforcement

During my research on the film, I also discovered something interesting in the comments on a YouTube reaction/review video by user @DanMurrellMovies (2023). Without realizing it, some audiences may interpret this perspective as a positive reinforcement of the white hegemony, unaware of the harm it causes by sidelining a person of color. Here, two comments spliced together for readability, capture opposing reactions: one commenter (@DelahayN) interprets Ben Affleck’s (the film’s director) decision not to show Jordan’s face as an artistic choice to elevate Jordan’s mystique, keeping his influence central without his presence. On the other hand, commenter @tallyprestin1881 critiques this choice, seeing it as problematic and arguing that it diminishes Jordan’s role, turning him into a mere backdrop for white characters who ultimately profit from his success.

These responses highlight the dual effect of the film’s artistic choices: for some, the omission of Jordan’s full presence seems intentional and clever, while for others, it underscores the reinforcement of white dominance by excluding the very person of color whose success and story should be central to the narrative.

Implications and Conclusion

In a gross overgeneralization on the theory of simulacra and simulation by Jean Baudrillard, where the imaginary of thing becomes the thing, race, constructed rather recently in the last few hundred years by those in power to define, and ultimately limit the other, has become an actualized part of identities and cultures around the world (1981). Although it began as words, as rhetoric, it became grafted to the skin of everyone in the human race.

Both the seemingly small choices, and large, from music selection to screen time, to positive representations of whiteness in a film ostensibly about the talent of, arguably, the best NBA player of all time, reinforced to both African-Americans, Blacks, Persons of Color, and white folks exactly what should matter: white culture, white music, and white agency.

Matt Damon and Ben Affleck, are generally liked, well received by critics, and rarely suffer from controversy, to say nothing of being accused of racism. Thus, how can such a well-intentioned film fall so short of the mark? It is because this pair of friends and actors are secretly racist? It is far more likely, instead, that they have simply existed in their whiteness, going about life benefiting in the inherent privilege of their whiteness. Critical media literacy demands, however, that we are vigilant, even with the most benign texts.

Even when media can be seen as benign, or historical, “merely” sympathetic, or “just facts” the results, the implications, the unintended consequences can be stark. In Race: Are We So Different?, the timeline points to D.W. Griffith’s Birth of a Nation (2020). Before the release of the film the Ku Klux Klan did not light crosses on fire and burn them in the yards of Black families. However, in an example of life imitating art, after the release of the film, the KKK began that specific ritual. Media cannot only reinforce systems of oppression, but in the worst case, worsen it, has history has shown.
