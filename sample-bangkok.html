Bangkok, Thailand – Observation and Discovery

Rich Riddle · Excerpt from Chapter 4 of my M.A. thesis on Thai BL, 2023

Why this piece

This excerpt comes from my M.A. thesis, an autoethnography about how Thai BL changed the way I see gender, religion, and my own country. In July 2022, I went to Thailand for the first time. This section records what I observed there: places and things, not people. It’s where my work in Thailand starts, and it’s work I plan to keep doing, reading Thai media and the culture around it without only using a US lens.

Ultimately as the world progressed through COVID, and I finished my undergraduate studies and began my career in academia as a student of rhetoric and composition, Thailand would eventually open itself up again to foreign visitors. It is here that Thai BL had a huge and outstanding impact on my life. Mr. Homebody himself would be traveling overseas for the first time, alone, to a new place, although as I found out—thanks to thousands of hours of Thai television—not entirely unfamiliar.

When I first arrived in Thailand in July 2022, I was surprised to see Thai BL stars on so many public advertisements. I saw them in the mall, on the sky train (an elevated rapid transit system officially called BTS), and on billboards and tuktuks alike. Shown below is the birthday wish for the actor “Nat” Natasitt Uareksit known for his roles in Thai BL series, like Why R U? (2020), Y-Destiny (2021), and Cutie Pie (2022). (The angle captured was not ideal, but the tuktuk was moving and I only had side access from the pedestrian sidewalk.)

Peter Jackson in 1999 discussed how Thailand addressed the HIV/AIDS epidemic. Although I acknowledge that prejudice against queer folk still exists in Thailand, I found Thai society was remarkably integrated in terms of protecting vulnerable populations. There seems to be a blend of both private interests and public systems that support sexual well-being in tandem. When I expressed interest in visiting Thailand to visit potential places to live and work in or around the gay district of Bangkok, sĕe lom (สีลม) (often written as Silom), Teacher Nick provided a list of suggestions. One that he insisted on was a favorite neighborhood restaurant, Cabbages & Condoms (CC). CC started in the 1970s as a humble vegetable stand that sold “vegetables, along with lace panties, T-shirts, key chains, condoms, and oral contraceptives.” It has supported safe sex and family planning for decades. It has expanded to different regions, and countries, but the original location in Bangkok still exists. As stated on their website, CC considers itself “a ‘Business for Social Progress’, and all profits support The Population & Community Development Association (PDA) and its programs in primary health, education, HIV/AIDS, rural development, environment and water etc.” It was shocking, almost, to my American eyes that a company in Thailand was so progressive.

I would later learn that CC’s founder, Mechai Viravaidya, aka Mr. Condom, also served in public roles such as senator, minister for “tourism, information and AIDS,” surviving, politically, through multiple military coups, and as part of an NGO. In 2007 Viravaidya’s organization, PDA, “was awarded the 2007 Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation's Gates Award [for Global Health] in recognition of its pioneering work in family planning and HIV/AIDS prevention. This award came with funds of $1,000,000” (Viravaidya). Perhaps if I had known in advance that Viravaidya was such a prolific advocate, I would have been less shocked to see a two-story digital billboard advocating for the LGBTQ+ community use of PrEP (a way to prevent the contraction of HIV) and condoms for the safest sex possible.

Having studied Thai language, Thai BL, and about Thailand in general from afar, it took going there to realize how very stuck in a US/Western view of the world—of the US and its “superior” nature—I truly was. Before going to Thailand, before even starting graduate school, I thought that perhaps I was just too much of a “fanboy” to ever look at the situation without bias. I discovered it was not so difficult.

It was humbling to realize how, in some ways, we—the great US—are much farther behind Thailand. Years behind. In 1991, a five-year-old Megan Phelps-Roper, a former member of the Westboro Baptist Church, held up a sign in the US that said “Gays Are Worthy of Death (ROM. 1:32)” (Padawer). I did not hold up that sign as a young child, but in the late 80s and early 90s I was taken to anti-abortion protests. I did hear the adults saying, with righteous indignation, that HIV/AIDS was God’s punishment on the homosexuals. While the US was embroiled in a health-religion-good-evil debate on HIV/AIDS, Thailand was actively focusing their energy on making progress. In a 2003 interview Mr. Condom is quoted as saying (regarding his role as minister for tourism, information, and AIDS):

So [in 1991] everyone that was involved in contact—be it in agriculture, be it in education, be it in interior—everyone had a role in passing on the message to the people in urban and rural settings. We have education in schools starting in primary school at fourth grade all the way up to the university, in the workplace, in the offices, in the villages. Gas stations were involved, helping to print AIDS information in what we called AIDS Safety Tips, and also giving out condoms. We had Avon ladies walking around selling their products with a special AIDS Safety Tips, printed by Avon, with the message of Avon on one side and knowledge about HIV/AIDS on the other side. Insurance companies, banks, everyone was involved. We even had policemen giving out condoms with parking tickets. We called that a Cops and Rubbers program. (Viravaidya 8)

Although on the skytrain I was noticeably taller, and wider, than the average Thai person as I reflected on some of our paralleled histories, I felt very small. I recalled the wonderful show opener by Will McAvoy (played by Jeff Daniels) in The Newsroom (2012). Presumptively asked why America was the greatest country in the world, he responded instead, “It’s not the greatest country in the world, professor, that’s my answer.” Two parts of his response stand out, in light of my trip and research:

…with a straight face, you're gonna tell students that America is so star-spangled awesome that we're the only ones in the world who have freedom? Canada has freedom. Japan has freedom. The UK, France, Italy, Germany, Spain, Australia, Belgium has freedom! So, 207 sovereign states in the world, like 180 of 'em have freedom.

McAvoy continues:

There is absolutely no evidence to support the statement that we're the greatest country in the world. We're 7th in literacy, 27th in math, 22nd in science, 49th in life expectancy, 178th in infant mortality, 3rd in median household income, number 4 in labor force and number 4 in exports. We lead the world in only three categories: number of incarcerated citizens per capita, number of adults who believe angels are real and defense spending—where we spend more than the next 26 countries combined, 25 of whom are allies…. So, when you ask what makes us the greatest country in the world, I don't know what the fuck you're talking about! Yosemite?

Although not perfect, in some ways, I found that Thailand was better at co-existing. Not without truth is it called the Land of Smiles; it is a reserved, polite, predominately conservative country with deep religious and cultural ties to Buddhism. But it is also a place where the social good matters. It is also, increasingly, a place where certain individuals speak out about the issues that matter to them. I found that in its own space—sometimes a literal space—Thai BL has asserted itself in very similar ways.

On my first full day in Bangkok, I wanted to see the GMM Grammy Building located in a region not too far from Thailand’s most well-known university, Chulalongkorn, as well as Thailand’s predominately gay district, sĕe lom, easily accessible and just a few meters from the Sala Daeng BTS Skytrain station. I also visited GMMTV, the leader in terms of number of series of Thai BL made. GMMTV is a television production and talent agent subsidiary of GMM Grammy, the largest media conglomerate in Thailand (Newell), and it was on my list of places to visit. It was rainy season, and I had walked a few kilometers to take in the sights, but I found the building and the 30th floor of the Grammy building where I could get my Thai BL merchandise fix. I would also, days later find Chinatown and its own collection of Thai BL merchandise.

A number of times since 2019 I’d seen all types of advertisements either as commercials baked in the first few seconds of a YouTube video, or obvious on-the-nose product placements, acted out as almost an aside by the characters—there are few casually placed items like in US television or Hollywood film, with its random can of Coke placed on a table or other types of seen-but-not-mentioned placement. Not in Thailand. Not with Thai BL, a genre that sometimes lacks deep pockets, especially outside of GMMTV productions.

But it was this “refreshing” drink that the men of Thai BL perpetually handed each other. I had a desperate desire to drink Oishi Honey, as advertised, a green tea, low in calorie and honey flavored. A product of Thailand, it is actually Japanese inspired (not unlike Thai BL) with other flavors—but I was only stuck on honey, a testament to the marketing power and brand placement in Thai BL. For approximately 20 baht (or approximately $0.60 USD), I certainly had my fill. However there were other components of Thai BL I could only see but not do.

Certain “scenes” from my trip surreally matched to what I had seen on TV. Friends walking from class—always in school uniform. Friends playing soccer—perhaps still in school uniform. Friends eating lunch, outdoors, by a canteen—obviously still in school uniforms. And certainly, the superiority of the Faculty (major) of Engineering to others, like the Faculty of Education. Although the differences in size are difficult to tell apart in the photos below—the proximity to which I could approach being a factor in distance and angle—the engineering sign is more than twice as large, adorned with both the country flag and the yellow flag, representing royalty and the king. Its shrubbery is also well maintained, unlike the sign for education. I’m not slighting the situation—but simply pointing out that Thai BL is a distributor of the state of things in Thailand and reflects the rhetorical decisions made to both me, and the broader international audience.
