The Grant Writer’s Toolkit: Twenty Principles

Rich Riddle · From a grant strategy audit for an undisclosed nonprofit, Fall 2025

Why this piece

I wrote this toolkit for a graduate course on grants and proposals for nonprofits, where I spent a semester auditing a nonprofit’s grant writing with a small research team. Our final package included a strategy memo, revisions to the organization’s active proposals, and these twenty principles, the part of that work any organization can use. It shows how I think about technical communication: a grant is a technical document written to persuade, and every part of it, down to the budget, makes an argument.

These twenty principles distill our audit findings into actionable strategies. They are designed to shift your approach from "asking for help" to "offering an investment opportunity."

Financial Rhetoric & Strategy

  1. The 85% Rule: Never request 100% of a grant’s funding cap unless you have a quote-backed, capital-intensive reason (like specialized equipment). Asking for the "ceiling" signals a lack of awareness of the competitive pool; aiming for ~85% (or the average award size) signals strategic precision and partnership.
  2. The Shadow Budget (In-Kind Leverage): Always quantify community support as financial assets. Funders want to match effort, not buy it entirely. List volunteer hours (valued at ~$33.49/hr) and donated space as "In-Kind Income" to prove the community has already "voted" for the project.
  3. The "Buy-Down" Strategy: Frame requests as "subsidizing" a specific, expensive fixed cost (like rent) to unlock organizational capacity. Instead of "we need money for operations," say "buying down our facility cost allows us to direct our earned revenue to program delivery," turning a bill into an investment.
  4. The Precision Principle: Avoid round numbers (e.g., $100,000 or $10,000) whenever possible. Round numbers look like estimates; exact numbers (e.g., $98,450) imply rigorous planning based on vendor quotes. Precision signals competence and fiscal discipline.
  5. The "Gap" Definition: Clearly distinguish between "Survival" (Base Funding) and "Growth" (Full Funding). Explicitly state what the marginal dollar buys: "A partial award sustains X; the full award unlocks Y." This prevents funders from cutting your request arbitrarily because they see the specific loss involved.
  6. The Payroll Signal: Budget is rhetoric. Listing core team members as "Contractors" (1099) signals instability and "gig economy" values. Listing them as "Staff" (W-2) signals institutional permanence and equity. Ensure your budget line items match your mission’s values on labor.
  7. The "Moonshot" Warning: Never project massive revenue increases (e.g., doubling individual donations) without a confirmed pipeline or a new dedicated resource. Funders view "hope-based budgeting" as a structural risk. Growth projections must be backed by data or new capacity.

Narrative & Framing

  1. The Mirroring Rule: Do not force the funder to translate your mission. Use their exact vocabulary. If they care about "Economic Mobility," frame your arts program as "Workforce Development" for creatives. If they care about "Preservation," talk about "Cultural Heritage," not just "Arts Education."
  2. The Right Hero (Leadership Alignment): Match the Project Lead to the Grant Category. For an arts grant, center the lead artist. For a technical or archival grant, center the Subject Matter Expert (e.g., the Archivist). The "face" of the proposal must possess the specific credentials the funder values most.
  3. The Urgency Trap: "Importance" is timeless; "Urgency" is time-bound. A successful proposal answers: "What is lost forever if this is not funded in the next 12 months?" (e.g., data degradation, loss of a lease, a retiring elder's story). Move from "we should do this" to "we must do this now."
  4. The SMARTIE Standard: Goals must be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound, Inclusive, and Equitable. Vague goals like "expand programming" result in denials. Specific goals like "pay 20 emerging artists $150 each to close the wealth gap" get funded.
  5. The "X out of Y" Metric: Avoid percentages without baselines (e.g., "increase attendance by 10%"). Use hard counts: "85% of 20 participants." Funders need to know the human scale of the impact—are you changing 5 lives deeply or touching 5,000 lives lightly?
  6. The Equity Frame: In 2026, "Equity" must be operational, not just aspirational. Explicitly mention pay rates, intellectual property rights, and accessibility measures to prove your work is non-extractive and that you are modeling the justice you seek.

Long-Term Sustainability

  1. The "Secret Santa" Protocol: For invitation-only funders, never apply cold. Launch a "Visibility Campaign" (e.g., send impact reports, invite to non-ask events) to trigger an invitation. Respect the gatekeepers and let your work earn the invite.
  2. Diversification Defense: If you rely heavily on one source (e.g., Government Grants), explicitly show how you are fixing it (e.g., "Launching a donor circle to increase individual giving to 30%"). Funders hate being the only lifeline; they want to be part of a balanced portfolio.
  3. The Cash Reserve Signal: Low interest income signals poor cash management. Show funders you respect money by maintaining interest-bearing reserves (High-Yield Savings), proving you are building a safety net for the organization's future.
  4. The "Cliff" Proof: Demonstrate that the project won't die when the grant ends. Show how the grant builds a permanent asset (equipment, curriculum, website) that generates value long after the check is cashed.
  5. The Strategic Candor Rule: “The first step in solving any problem is recognizing there is one.” Do not hide organizational weaknesses (such as a lost major grant). Acknowledge them directly and position the new grant as the intentional, strategic solution to that specific gap. Being candid strengthens credibility and builds trust with stakeholders.
  6. The Rubric Supremacy Rule: Follow the rubric over the passion of the project. Passion captures attention, but the rubric calculates the award. If the rubric gives 10 points for "metrics," give them metrics, even if it feels dry. Score yourself honestly against the rubric before you submit.
  7. The Rhetorical Opportunity: Every weakness is a narrative pivot point. A deficit is an "investment opportunity." A small staff is "lean efficiency." A lack of space is a "readiness to scale." Frame every challenge not as a failure, but as the exact reason the funder’s intervention will yield a high return on investment.
